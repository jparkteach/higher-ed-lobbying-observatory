What the updated data say

Research dependence retains a positive cross-sectional association. In the pooled reconstruction, a +10 percentage point difference in federal R&D dependence is associated with 4.06 times the odds of ever lobbying (95% CI 1.79–9.22; p <0.001) and $100,520 higher average annual spending (95% CI $56,149 to $144,891; p <0.001), holding Camp’s controls constant.

The for-profit Pell hypothesis is not supported by the sector-specific estimates. For +10 percentage points of Pell dependence, the for-profit-only odds ratio is 0.77 (95% CI 0.37–1.62; p 0.489) and the spending coefficient is −$1,909 (95% CI −$7,540 to $3,721; p 0.506). Only 19 of 275 as-supplied for-profit reporting entities ever lobby, so precision is limited. Lack of statistical evidence does not establish an absence of any effect.

The annual spending result is less stable. In the pooled annual model with year indicators and entity-clustered uncertainty, the R&D spending interval crosses zero. Annual registration remains positively associated with R&D dependence. Cross-sectional and annual models answer different questions; neither identifies a causal effect.

The file sums to $343,304,475 nominal spending over 2015–2022, or $391,978,188 after conversion to 2022 dollars. These are as-supplied sums, not independently deduplicated filing totals. Among 1,850 reporting groups, 352 (19.0%) ever have observed lobbying. Do not interpret the difference from Camp’s reported 37% as a clean temporal decline: populations, reporting groups and exposure windows differ.

What Camp did and found

Camp’s No Longer Exempt: Higher Education’s Entrée into Lobbying (Columbia University, 2021) combines resource-dependence theory, academic capitalism and institutional imitation to examine whether colleges lobby like corporations. Chapter 3 merges IPEDS finances and characteristics, NSF research data and lobbying disclosures for 2004–2014. It recombines campuses into systems and estimates logistic models for registration and OLS models for spending, for all institutions, standalones and systems (Tables 20–21). Camp reports weak evidence connecting for-profit lobbying to Pell and stronger evidence connecting nonprofit lobbying to research dependence.

Chapter 4 adds 20 interviews with New York lobbyists, legislators and staff, plus roughly 200 coded news articles. Its findings concern interdependent federal/state resources, increasingly specific coalitions, accountability pressures and a mixture of direct and indirect tactics. His overall conclusion is nuanced: colleges display some corporate patterns and some distinct institutional practices. The CSV can update the quantitative portion, not recreate these qualitative findings.

Concrete potential overlap: Michigan

In 2015, unit 170976 (University of Michigan–Ann Arbor) has $295,000 direct spending. Units 171137 (Dearborn) and 171146 (Flint), assigned to “University of Michigan,” have $157,496.57 + $137,503.43 = $295,000 allocated spending. The file’s sum is therefore $590,000 across those records. Similar equality recurs in later years. This is a candidate overlapping filing, not proof of duplication: the underlying reports are needed to distinguish system-wide and campus-specific amounts. No amount was silently removed.

Reporting labels also fragment institutions over time. For example, Cornell’s unit 190415 is assigned to its numeric campus ID before 2018 and “Cornell University” thereafter. The primary reconstruction therefore contains two source reporting groups for that campus. Their cross-sectional outcomes should not be read as two independent universities. The stable-family sensitivity links such labels through shared campus IDs, but cannot resolve Michigan’s issue because its separate groups do not share a campus ID.

Needed before scholarly publication: validate filing-level matches and allocations; establish a consistent crosswalk for systems, campuses and ownership changes; confirm the dataset’s nominal/real dollar basis and fiscal-year alignment. Title IV eligibility, degree offerings and exclusions used in Camp’s original sample cannot all be verified from these 30 columns. State coverage is the 50 states and D.C.; the supplied panel is otherwise analyzed as provided.

Reconstruction choices and model definitions

This is an extension to a new period, not a reproduction of Camp’s original numeric estimates. His original data and statistical code were not supplied. Where the dissertation leaves implementation details ambiguous, the following conventions are explicit and reproducible.

  1. Unit: primary annual records combine campuses sharing the same supplied system_group. A group equal to its numeric campus ID is a standalone; any other label is a system, even if only one member appears. Groups may represent corporate owners or reporting umbrellas, not necessarily independent universities.
  2. Money: all money fields are treated as nominal and multiplied by CPI-U(2022)/CPI-U(year). Budget, Pell, R&D and spending use the same factor. BLS annual CPI-U, U.S. city average, all items supplies the index. If the original money fields were already deflated, this assumption must be changed; nominal annual spending is separately available.
  3. Annual grouping: sum member-campus money and enrollment; take the maximum lobbied indicator and each characteristic flag. Shared finance reporting is not reconstructed. A system with some missing campus budgets is retained if its combined budget is positive; complete-campus-budget sensitivity excludes any entity with flagged missing member finances in any observed year.
  4. Temporal aggregation: one row per supplied reporting group over 2015–2022. Outcome A is ever lobbied; outcome B is mean annual real spending over that group’s observed years, including confirmed zero years. Enrollment is the average annual system total. Budget is the mean annual real budget over positive-budget years. Pell and R&D dependence are ratios of the sums of real funding to real budget over those same positive-budget years. Missing years are not imputed as zeros.
  5. Characteristics: any observed member-campus/year with a flag makes the cross-sectional group flag 1. Sector is the most common annual sector, latest year breaking ties. This follows Camp’s ever-characteristic convention but cannot reproduce his hand-corrected historical exceptions.
  6. Models: unrestricted logistic maximum likelihood for lobbying, OLS in dollars for spending, with the same 12 predictors as Tables 20–21. Public is the sector reference. Both funding ratios enter per +10 percentage points, FTE undergraduate enrollment per 1,000, and budget per $100 million in 2022 dollars. OLS includes non-lobbyists and is not a conditional-on-positive-spending model. Constant or exactly collinear terms are removed in restricted sector models and this is disclosed in diagnostics.
  7. Inference: HC0 sandwich uncertainty for cross-sectional logit and HC3 for cross-sectional OLS. Conventional standard errors and p-values are also exported for comparison with Camp’s reported tables. Annual extensions add year indicators and cluster by the supplied reporting group; they do not include institution fixed effects. Bernoulli cluster scores are computed as X(y−p) to remain numerically stable for fitted probabilities close to 0 or 1. Cluster covariance uses CR1 finite-sample correction; logit intervals are normal-based, OLS intervals use the cluster degrees of freedom.
  8. Interpretation: odds ratios refer to odds, not probabilities. Funding differences are absolute percentage-point differences, not relative percent changes. Camp’s printed narrative and table ratio units are not always aligned; this reconstruction reports units consistently rather than inheriting the narrative conversions. Associations are not lobbying returns or evidence that lobbying causes grant awards.
logit Pr(ever lobbyᵢ) = α + β₁(R&D shareᵢ / 10) + β₂(Pell shareᵢ / 10) + sector indicators + institutional flags + enrollmentᵢ / 1,000 + budgetᵢ / $100m

mean annual lobbyingᵢ = the same right-hand-side predictors + errorᵢ

Annual extensions replace outcomes and predictors with entity-year values and add year indicators.

Statistical implementation: statsmodels binomial generalized linear model and OLS. Conditional estimates depend on the measurement and grouping conventions above.

  • Annual panel: registration’s positive R&D association survives year indicators and clustered uncertainty; pooled and standalone annual spending intervals include zero. The system-only annual spending estimate is positive. This qualifies an unreserved claim that research dependence predicts spending in every specification.
  • Complete campus budgets: omitting entities with any member-campus missing-budget flag leaves the main pattern similar, but cannot resolve shared finance reporting.
  • Mean annual ratios: using a mean of annual ratios rather than a ratio of sums changes magnitudes; the main positive R&D pattern remains. This matters because revenue denominators can be volatile.
  • Stable reporting families: link all supplied group labels that share at least one campus ID during observed years, then reaggregate yearly totals and reconstruct the cross section. This prevents a changing campus label from creating multiple historical families. It can also join systems through acquisitions and does not assert current common ownership. The positive R&D cross-sectional pattern remains, but filing overlap can persist.
  • ≤100% funding ratios: all main cross-sectional shares already satisfy this rule, so this check removes no observations. A selected shorter dashboard window can still have ratios above 100%; its scatterplot control discloses omitted points.

The sensitivity checks do not correct unidentified overlapping disclosures, campus ownership histories or incomplete financial reporting. Thus, the overall evidence is consistent with Camp’s research-dependence pattern and provides no clear new support for H1, conditional on this dataset and the stated choices.

Five visualization choices

  1. Annual sector trends: compare nominal or constant-dollar spending and annual registration rates; pending years are explicit, and the 2007/2011 policy effects are not inferred.
  2. Institutional profiles: contrast ever-lobbying prevalence with and without Camp’s controls and an MSI extension. These are unadjusted and overlapping groups.
  3. For-profit Pell scatter: inspect RQ3 using every eligible reporting group, including zeros; Pearson correlation uses unlogged dollars while the vertical display uses log(1 + dollars).
  4. Nonprofit research scatter: inspect RQ4, with colors by sector and point sizes indicating source system status. Institution drilldowns retain annual detail.
  5. Coefficient forest: compare all/standalone/system models, sector-specific tests, annual extensions and sensitivities with confidence intervals. Models remain fixed at their labeled 2015–2022 samples, independent of exploration filters.
